<div class="leadership-intro">

I've spent my career moving between hands-on engineering and leading technical teams. Along the way, I've learned that developing people and delivering business outcomes shouldn't be competing priorities. The better we develop our people, the more capable our organization becomes. And the stronger the organization becomes, the more opportunities we can create for those people.

I genuinely care about the people in my charge. I want to understand what motivates them, what they're good at, and what might be holding them back. But leadership also means protecting our customers, the commitments we make, and the capacity of the team.

Five principles guide how I lead people. Another question guides how I make decisions across the organization: **Does It Scale?**

</div>

<div class="principle-details">

<section class="leadership-chapter" id="principle-01" aria-labelledby="1-develop-people-not-just-results" tabindex="-1">

### 1. Develop People, Not Just Results

I believe there's something great in everyone. Sometimes they know what it is. Sometimes they haven't discovered it yet.

Maybe they've never been pushed in the right way. Maybe they haven't learned how they work or learn best. Maybe a previous leader made it uncomfortable to ask questions. I've seen what that kind of leadership does to people, and I never wanted to lead that way.

I try to see what someone can become, not just where they are today. Sometimes that means coaching through a difficult conversation. Sometimes it's finding the right stretch assignment, introducing a different way of working, or creating the opportunity for someone to prove to themselves that they're capable of more.

I've seen the effect a strong example can have on a team. Bringing in an engineer who demonstrated what deeper technical engagement could look like helped others see a different standard. Just as important, it showed our sales stakeholders that the SE organization could engage differently, respond, and earn their confidence.

<p class="leadership-related"><a href="#coaching">How this works in practice: Coach Up, Not Out →</a></p>

Seeing someone earn a promotion or step into a responsibility they once doubted they could handle is one of the most rewarding parts of leadership for me. Their success is my success.

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter" id="principle-02" aria-labelledby="2-build-confidence" tabindex="-1">

### 2. Build Confidence

Confidence is one of the greatest tools an SE can develop. I see confidence in yourself, confidence in the product, and confidence with customers as closely connected.

If you doubt your own ability to solve a problem, it becomes difficult to convince a customer you can help. And if you don't trust that the product can deliver what you're promising, that uncertainty eventually comes through.

I've been in customer conversations where confidence in the solution and a clear understanding of the customer's problem mattered more than another demonstration or a deeper list of features. The important thing wasn't putting on a performance. It was helping the customer believe we understood their needs and could deliver responsibly.

There is a fine line between confidence and arrogance. Customers need to feel that we're looking out for them, not simply trying to sell them something. That's why I want SEs who can speak with conviction, listen carefully, acknowledge limits, and say, "I don't know. Let me validate that."

Confidence should grow through preparation, experience, product knowledge, and judgment. It shouldn't require pretending to know every answer.

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter" id="principle-03" aria-labelledby="3-stay-curious-and-keep-learning" tabindex="-1">

### 3. Stay Curious and Keep Learning

"Why?" is one of the most useful questions I've learned to ask.

Why does this customer need that particular configuration? Why does this process exist? Why do we keep encountering the same problem? What are we really trying to accomplish?

When people understand the why, they have a reason to care about the outcome. They can see the benefit of doing the work, not just the instruction to complete it.

I've always enjoyed working through technical problems myself. As my leadership responsibilities grew, I learned that personally solving every problem doesn't scale. The decision to step in depends on time, urgency, and impact. A critical customer issue may require immediate involvement. A less urgent situation can become an opportunity for an engineer to investigate and grow.

Sometimes the right move is simply, "Hey, can you take the first stab at this?" I can still coach from the side while they own the work, build confidence, and learn how to make the next decision without me.

I want to remain technically curious, but I don't want my team to depend on me for every technical answer. The more we learn and teach, the more independently we can operate.

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter" id="principle-04" aria-labelledby="4-remove-friction-before-adding-process" tabindex="-1">

### 4. Remove Friction Before Adding Process

A lot of my career has involved finding ways to simplify work, automate repetition, and make technical organizations more effective. I also believe **some friction is good friction**.

Reviews, standard timelines, clear ownership, and escalation paths exist for a reason. They protect technical quality, establish expectations, and make the organization predictable. The problem isn't the existence of a process. It's when the process creates more work than value, or when constant exceptions make the standard meaningless.

If an activity normally requires two days, I want sales and engineering to understand that expectation and plan around it. If a real business need requires something faster, there should be an exception path that explains why the change matters and who is accountable for the decision.

This is also a leader-to-leader responsibility. When other leaders understand why a process exists and what it protects, they can help filter unnecessary requests and support justified exceptions before the burden falls on the SE team.

I don't want to remove discipline to make things feel easier in the moment. I want to remove unnecessary friction while preserving the controls that allow teams to execute consistently.

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter" id="principle-05" aria-labelledby="5-accountability-starts-with-clarity" tabindex="-1">

### 5. Accountability Starts With Clarity

People deserve to know what's expected of them, why it matters, and how their performance affects their teammates, customers, and the business.

When an engineer is underperforming, I first want to understand why. It could be product knowledge, confidence, motivation, unclear expectations, poor role fit, or some combination. The response depends on the person and the situation.

A pooled SE team may have more flexibility to cover work while someone develops. A dedicated SE supporting a single sales representative can create a more immediate business impact when performance slips. That changes how quickly I need to provide support, establish coverage, and make decisions. It shouldn't change the obligation to be clear, fair, or honest.

I invest time, work with the person on goals, and stay engaged. Sometimes I step in personally to protect a stakeholder relationship while that person works through a difficult period. I'm willing to defend someone's opportunity to improve, but that opportunity needs to be grounded in progress and the requirements of the role.

Accountability applies to me, too. I should be transparent about what is happening, what I'm doing about it, and what the business can expect next. Problems should be handled at the appropriate level, not hidden.

</section>

<p class="eyebrow">03 / The operating question</p>

## Does It Scale?

<div class="framework-layout">
<div class="framework-context">

This is probably one of the questions I've asked most often throughout my career.

**Does it scale?**

Every decision comes with a tax on some form of capacity. A small request may consume an SE's time. A special configuration may create a long-term support obligation. A one-off customer promise can become an expectation across an entire sales organization.

The real question isn't just whether we *can* do something. It's what happens after we do it.

I want engineers and leaders to ask:

</div>
<div class="framework-questions">

1. **Why are we doing this?** What customer or business outcome does it support?
2. **Are we the right people to do it?** Does it belong with Presales, the partner, Support, Professional Services, or another team?
3. **What's the total cost?** Consider time, other opportunities, follow-on support, and the expectation we create.
4. **What happens when the next customer asks?** Is the work repeatable, transferable, or intentionally an exception?
5. **Is the value worth the decision?** Sometimes the most scalable answer is simply yes, because arguing over a small request would cost more than completing it.

</div>
</div>
<div class="framework-closing">

This question matters in Commercial, where limited resources need to serve many opportunities. It also matters in the upper market, where specialized customer requirements can create even larger commitments. Scaling doesn't mean always saying no to customization. It means making the tradeoff deliberately, with ownership and a business reason.

</div>

## Technical Integrity. Commercial Outcomes.

<div class="practice-intro">

I don't see technical integrity as something separate from commercial success. It's part of what makes commercial success sustainable.

If we misposition a capability or promise that an unsupported configuration will work, the problem doesn't disappear when the purchase order arrives. It lands on the customer, Support, Professional Services, and often the same SEs who should be helping the next customer. That costs trust, capacity, and future opportunity.

Sometimes the customer's stated technical requirement isn't the underlying problem.

</div>
<section class="leadership-case" aria-label="An anonymous SASE customer engagement">

### Situation

In one SASE engagement, a customer demanded a complete proof-of-concept deployment even after the requirements were identified, solutions accepted, demonstrations completed, and a hands-on lab finished. The request didn't seem to be about proving one more technical feature. We kept asking why the full deployment was necessary.

The concern was what would happen *after* the purchase. The customer worried that the vendor would disappear once the order was signed.

### Decision & action

Rather than run an additional full PoC, we brought internal Professional Services into the conversation, scoped the work, developed a Statement of Work, and introduced the Customer Success team. That made implementation ownership and continued engagement concrete.

### Outcome & lesson

The deal closed without the additional PoC, and the customer purchased additional Professional Services.

The win wasn't doing less because the team wanted to avoid work. It was understanding the actual obstacle, making a supportable commitment, and improving the commercial outcome.

</section>
<section class="leadership-coaching" id="coaching" aria-labelledby="coach-up-not-out" tabindex="-1">

### Coach Up, Not Out

When someone is struggling, my starting point is **Coach Up, Not Out**. Someone saw potential in that person when they were hired. I want to understand what changed, invest in their development, set clear goals, and stand beside them while they work to improve.

That doesn't mean avoiding difficult decisions. Expectations, role fit, and the impact on others matter. A fair chance to succeed must come with honest feedback and a reasonable timeframe.

</section>

<p class="eyebrow">05 / Leader to leader</p>

## Leader to Leader: No Surprises

<div class="transparency-copy">

I want senior leaders and my peers to have confidence that I know what's happening on the ground. I am open in my one-on-ones about the challenges we're seeing, how I'm handling them, and where risk may be developing.

If I get a hint that an issue could reach them, I'd rather they hear it from me first. That doesn't mean every problem needs to be escalated for someone else to solve. It means I resolve what belongs at my level while ensuring other leaders are never surprised by something material.

Transparency builds trust. So does follow-through. The goal is to have managers who can operate independently, leaders who are aligned on expectations, and stakeholders who know that concerns will be addressed without constant intervention.

</div>
<section class="leadership-success" id="leadership-success" aria-labelledby="what-success-looks-like" tabindex="-1">

### What Success Looks Like

I want to build teams that are capable of making good decisions without needing me in the middle of every conversation.

I want managers who develop their own people, engineers who take ownership, and sales relationships built on mutual trust and respect. I want processes that create predictability without slowing every decision down. And I want customers to receive what we've confidently committed to deliver.

I'm proud when a team performs well. I'm even prouder when someone grows into a bigger role, earns a promotion, or discovers a capability they didn't know they had.

That, to me, is leadership... creating the conditions for people to succeed, building the trust to operate independently, and delivering outcomes that keep working after the immediate problem is solved.
