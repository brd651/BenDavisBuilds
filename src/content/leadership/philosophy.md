<div class="leadership-introduction">

I’ve worked on both sides of technical leadership: solving engineering problems myself and leading the people responsible for solving them. In sales engineering, those responsibilities meet in the middle. We need to help customers make good technical decisions, support the business, and build teams that are capable of doing both consistently.

I don't see developing people and delivering results as competing priorities. Stronger engineers make better decisions. Better decisions build trust with customers and sales partners. And a stronger organization creates more opportunities for its people.

</div>

<section class="leadership-origin" id="leadership-origin" aria-labelledby="where-my-leadership-began" tabindex="-1">
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">The foundation / U.S. Marine Corps NCO</p>

## Where My Leadership Began

</div>
<div class="origin-copy">

My foundation in leadership began as a noncommissioned officer in the United States Marine Corps. The setting was different from the technical organizations I would later lead, but the lessons carried forward: know your people, set clear expectations, take responsibility, and communicate early when something could put the team or mission at risk.

I don't think military leadership should simply be copied into a business environment. I've had to adapt how I lead as the people, customers, and problems changed. The underlying commitment to accountability, trust, and developing others has stayed with me.

</div>
</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter principle-01" id="principle-01" aria-labelledby="1-develop-people-not-just-results" tabindex="-1">
<header class="principle-heading">
<span class="chapter-number" aria-hidden="true">01</span>
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">People / potential</p>
<h3 id="1-develop-people-not-just-results">Develop People, Not Just Results</h3>
<p class="chapter-summary">Opportunity, support, and confidence.</p>
</div>
</header>
<div class="chapter-composition">

<div class="chapter-main">

I believe there's something great in everyone. Sometimes people know where their strengths lie. Sometimes they haven't had the right opportunity, support, or confidence to discover them.

I want to understand what motivates someone, how they learn, where they want to go, and what might be standing in their way. Development might mean helping someone prepare for a difficult customer conversation, assigning work that stretches their abilities, or creating an opportunity to lead something they haven't led before.

</div>
<blockquote class="chapter-pull">

I've also seen how one strong example can raise the standard for a team. Bringing in an engineer who modeled deeper technical engagement gave colleagues a different picture of what was possible and helped sales stakeholders see what they could expect from the SE organization.

</blockquote>

</div>

<div class="chapter-rest">

I care about results, but seeing someone grow into a role or responsibility they once doubted they could handle is one of the most rewarding parts of leadership for me.

</div>

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter principle-02" id="principle-02" aria-labelledby="2-build-confidence" tabindex="-1">
<header class="principle-heading">
<span class="chapter-number" aria-hidden="true">02</span>
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">Trust / preparation</p>
<h3 id="2-build-confidence">Build Confidence</h3>
<p class="chapter-summary">Preparation, product knowledge, experience, and judgment.</p>
</div>
</header>
<div class="chapter-composition">

<div class="chapter-main">

Confidence is one of the most useful tools a sales engineer can develop. It comes from knowing your craft, understanding the customer's problem, and being able to stand behind the solution you're recommending.

</div>
<blockquote class="chapter-pull">

A confident SE doesn't need to know every answer. They need to ask good questions, explain what they know, acknowledge what they don't, and follow through. Saying “I don't know. Let me validate that” often builds more trust than a confident answer that turns out to be wrong.

</blockquote>

</div>

<div class="chapter-rest">

I've been in customer conversations where understanding the real concern and giving a credible answer mattered more than another demonstration or feature list. That kind of confidence isn't a performance. It's earned through preparation, product knowledge, experience, and judgment.

I want SEs who can speak with conviction without crossing the line into arrogance and who never confuse a sales commitment with a technically supportable one.

</div>

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter principle-03" id="principle-03" aria-labelledby="3-stay-curious-and-keep-learning" tabindex="-1">
<header class="principle-heading">
<span class="chapter-number" aria-hidden="true">03</span>
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">Curiosity / independence</p>
<h3 id="3-stay-curious-and-keep-learning">Stay Curious and Keep Learning</h3>
<p class="chapter-summary">Build independent judgment across the team.</p>
</div>
</header>
<div class="chapter-composition">

<blockquote class="chapter-pull">

“Why?” is one of the most valuable questions we can ask.

</blockquote>
<div class="chapter-main">

Why does the customer need a particular configuration? Why does a process exist? Why are we solving the same problem repeatedly? What outcome are we actually trying to achieve?

</div>

</div>

<div class="chapter-rest">

I'm still drawn to hands-on engineering, but leadership has taught me that personally solving every technical problem doesn't scale. Sometimes the situation is urgent enough that I need to step in. Other times, the better decision is to let an engineer take the first pass and coach them while they own the work.

I want to stay technically credible without becoming the person everyone depends on for an answer. The goal is to build independent judgment across the team, not centralize it in the manager.

</div>

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter principle-04" id="principle-04" aria-labelledby="4-remove-friction-before-adding-process" tabindex="-1">
<header class="principle-heading">
<span class="chapter-number" aria-hidden="true">04</span>
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">Discipline / simplicity</p>
<h3 id="4-remove-friction-before-adding-process">Remove Friction Before Adding Process</h3>
<p class="chapter-summary">Protect quality and remove unnecessary friction.</p>
</div>
</header>
<div class="chapter-composition">

<div class="chapter-main">

I've spent a meaningful part of my career finding ways to simplify work, automate repetitive tasks, and make technical organizations more effective. But I don't believe that all process is bad.

</div>
<blockquote class="chapter-pull">

Reviews, clear ownership, reasonable turnaround times, and escalation paths can protect quality and help teams make reliable commitments. The issue is when a process creates more work than value, or when constant exceptions make the standard meaningless.

</blockquote>

</div>

<div class="chapter-rest">

If an activity normally takes two days, sales and engineering should know that and plan around it. When there's a real business reason to move faster, we need a clear exception path, not an unspoken expectation that the team can absorb every urgent request.

Part of my job is helping other leaders understand why the process exists and when an exception makes sense. The objective is not to remove discipline. It's to remove unnecessary friction while protecting the controls that let teams execute consistently.

</div>

</section>

<section class="leadership-chapter principle-05" id="principle-05" aria-labelledby="5-accountability-starts-with-clarity" tabindex="-1">
<header class="principle-heading">
<span class="chapter-number" aria-hidden="true">05</span>
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">Clarity / ownership</p>
<h3 id="5-accountability-starts-with-clarity">Accountability Starts With Clarity</h3>
<p class="chapter-summary">Clear goals, support, and honest feedback.</p>
</div>
</header>
<div class="chapter-composition">

<div class="chapter-main">

People deserve to know what good performance looks like, why it matters, and how their work affects their teammates, customers, and the business.

When someone is struggling, I first want to understand the cause. Is it knowledge, confidence, motivation, expectations, role fit, or something else? Then we can agree on the support needed, set clear goals, and determine what progress should look like.

The business context matters, too. In a pooled SE model, the team may have room to provide coverage while someone develops. A dedicated SE working closely with one sales representative can create a more immediate customer or sales impact when performance slips. That can change the urgency of the response, but not the obligation to be fair and clear.

I'll invest the time, coach directly, and step in when necessary to protect a relationship. I also owe the person honest feedback about whether the needed progress is happening.

</div>
<blockquote class="chapter-pull">

Accountability applies to me as well. My peers and leaders should understand the issues, the plan, and what they can expect next.

</blockquote>

</div>

</section>

## Does It Scale?

</div>
<div class="framework-opening">

This is one of the questions I've returned to throughout my career.

A seemingly small request can consume engineering capacity, create a future support obligation, or establish an expectation that gets repeated across an entire sales organization. Being able to do something isn't the same as making a good decision to do it.

</div>
</header>

Before committing the team, I ask five questions:

<div class="framework-questions">
<ol>

<li><span class="decision-index" aria-hidden="true">01</span><div class="decision-copy"><strong>Why are we doing this?</strong><p>What customer or business outcome does it support?</p></div></li>

<li><span class="decision-index" aria-hidden="true">02</span><div class="decision-copy"><strong>Are we the right owners?</strong><p>Should this sit with Presales, a partner, Support, Professional Services, or another team?</p></div></li>

<li><span class="decision-index" aria-hidden="true">03</span><div class="decision-copy"><strong>What&#x27;s the full cost?</strong><p>Consider time, displaced opportunities, follow-on support, and the expectation created.</p></div></li>

<li><span class="decision-index" aria-hidden="true">04</span><div class="decision-copy"><strong>What happens when the next customer asks?</strong><p>Is this repeatable, transferable, or intentionally an exception?</p></div></li>

<li><span class="decision-index" aria-hidden="true">05</span><div class="decision-copy"><strong>Is it worth it?</strong><p>Sometimes the right answer is yes. A small request may cost less to complete than to debate.</p></div></li>

</ol>
</div>
<div class="framework-notes">

Scaling doesn't mean refusing customization or always choosing the cheapest path. It means making deliberate tradeoffs with clear ownership and an understanding of the downstream impact.

</div>
</section>

<section class="leadership-practice" id="technical-integrity-and-business-outcomes" aria-labelledby="technical-integrity-commercial-outcomes" tabindex="-1">
<p class="eyebrow">Leadership in practice / an anonymous SASE engagement</p>

## Technical Integrity. Commercial Outcomes.

<div class="practice-intro">

I don't separate technical integrity from commercial success. One of the fastest ways to create future problems is to promise a customer something the product or delivery organization can't support.

That issue may not surface until after the purchase order. By then, it affects the customer's trust, Support, Professional Services, and the SEs who should be focused on helping the next customer.

</div>
<div class="case-opening">

### A SASE engagement: finding the problem behind the request

</div>
<dl class="leadership-case" aria-label="An anonymous SASE customer engagement">

<div class="case-stage case-stage-01"><dt><span aria-hidden="true">01</span>Situation</dt><dd>

A customer continued asking for a full proof-of-concept deployment after requirements had been examined, solutions accepted, demonstrations completed, and a hands-on lab finished. We needed to understand what the additional deployment would actually prove.

</dd></div>

<div class="case-stage case-stage-02"><dt><span aria-hidden="true">02</span>What we learned</dt><dd>

The concern wasn't another technical feature. The customer wanted confidence that they wouldn't be left on their own after purchasing the solution.

</dd></div>

<div class="case-stage case-stage-03"><dt><span aria-hidden="true">03</span>What we did</dt><dd>

Instead of running a redundant full deployment, we brought Professional Services into the discussion, scoped the implementation work, developed a Statement of Work, and introduced the Customer Success team. That made the post-sale plan and ownership tangible.

</dd></div>

<div class="case-stage case-stage-04"><dt><span aria-hidden="true">04</span>Outcome</dt><dd>

The deal closed without the additional PoC, and the customer purchased additional Professional Services.

</dd></div>

</dl>
<div class="case-lesson">

The lesson wasn't to avoid technical work. It was to keep asking questions until we understood the real obstacle, then make a commitment we could support.

</div>
</section>

<section class="leadership-coaching" data-theme="dark" id="coaching" aria-labelledby="coach-up-not-out" tabindex="-1">
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">A featured idea / development with clarity</p>

## Coach Up, Not Out

</div>
<div class="coaching-copy">

When someone is struggling, my starting point is to understand how to help them succeed, not immediately look for a replacement.

That means identifying the gap, giving specific feedback, setting reasonable expectations, and following through on a development plan. I don't consider encouragement alone to be coaching; people need clarity, practice, and a fair way to show progress.

A fair opportunity to improve doesn't mean avoiding a difficult decision indefinitely. The individual deserves honesty, and the rest of the team and the business deserve consistency. Good leadership requires both.

</div>
</section>

<section class="leadership-transparency" id="leadership-transparency" aria-labelledby="leader-to-leader-no-surprises" tabindex="-1">
<div>
<p class="eyebrow">Transparency and ownership</p>
<h2 id="leader-to-leader-no-surprises"><span class="transparency-context">Leader to Leader:</span> No Surprises</h2>
<span id="no-surprises" aria-hidden="true"></span>
</div>
<div class="transparency-copy">

I'm very open with my peers and senior leaders about what's happening on the ground, what we're doing about it, and where risks may be emerging.

If I see early signs of an issue that could reach them, I want them to hear it from me first. I don't wait for a problem to become urgent or for someone else to bring it to their attention.

But proactive communication isn't the same thing as passing the problem upward. I should handle what belongs at my level, keep ownership of the response, and give other leaders enough context that they're never blindsided by something material.

I want my managers to operate with that same judgment: independently enough to resolve problems, transparently enough that we stay aligned, and thoughtfully enough to know when another leader needs to be involved.

</div>
</section>

<section class="leadership-success" id="leadership-success" aria-labelledby="what-success-looks-like" tabindex="-1">
<p class="eyebrow">The measure / people who can own the next decision</p>

## What Success Looks Like

<div class="success-copy">

I want to build teams that make good decisions without needing me in the middle of every conversation.

That means managers who develop people, engineers who take ownership, sales partners who trust the technical organization, processes that support execution, and customers who receive what we committed to deliver.

I'm proud of strong team performance. I'm just as proud when someone grows into a larger role or discovers a capability they didn't realize they had.

</div>
<blockquote class="success-statement">

**To me, successful leadership is creating the conditions for people to grow, make sound decisions, and deliver outcomes that continue to work without constant intervention.**

</blockquote>
</section>
